Every Push & Pull
Around You

Kicking a ball, opening a door, lifting a bag — they're all the same thing: a force. Master what a force can do, how we measure it, and why direction matters, all by playing with it.

▶ Start learning Test myself first
1The big idea

A force is just a push or a pull

That's the whole definition. Lifting a box, opening a door, kicking a ball, dragging a cart — in every case you're either pushing or pulling something. Science calls that a force.

But a force doesn't always cause motion

Push a book on a table and it slides. Kick a ball and it flies. But push a wall as hard as you like — nothing moves. One person can't push a bus either. So a force is applied in both cases; whether the object moves depends on the object.

Reframe itDon't think "force = movement." Think "force = an attempt (a push or pull)." Sometimes that attempt causes motion, stops motion, or just changes the object's shape. That's exactly what the next section explores.

3Magnitude of force

How big is the force? (magnitude)

Hit a ball gently and it rolls slowly; hit it hard and it races off. So a force has a size — its magnitude. We measure it with a spring balance.

The spring balance — pull it and see

Inside a spring balance is a spring that stretches more when the force is bigger. The scale is marked (calibrated) by how far the spring stretches. Drag the slider to pull with different forces:

Simulator · Spring balance

3.0 N
Bigger pull → the spring stretches more → higher reading. Stretch is proportional to force.
The unit of forceIn the international system (SI), force is measured in Newtons (N). School spring balances read in Newtons; shop ones often read in grams or kilograms.
Weight is a force tooWhen you hang a stone on a spring balance, the reading is its weight — the gravitational force the Earth pulls it with. So weight is measured in Newtons, not kilograms!
Using it correctlyTo measure the force needed to move a block, pull the spring balance horizontally until the block just starts to move, then read it. Hold it in line with the motion.
4Direction & point of application

Where and which way you push matters

A force isn't just a size. Which way you push (direction) and where on the object you push (point of application) both change the result.

Direction: the object moves the way you push

Open a drawer — you pull it towards you. Close it — you push it away. The object moves along the direction of the force. Whichever way the thread is pulled, that's the way the block slides.

Point of application: same push, different result

The point of an object on which the force is exerted is the point of application. Push a tall bottle near the bottom and it slides; push near the top and it topples. Same force, different point → totally different outcome. Try it:

5Force is a vector

Two things define a force: size + direction

A force always has both a magnitude and a direction. Quantities that need both to be fully described are called vector quantities. So —

Force is a vector quantity.

magnitude (how strong)  +  direction (which way)

Why it mattersSaying "a force of 10 N" is incomplete — 10 N which way? A push of 10 N to the right and 10 N to the left do opposite things. A vector carries both facts at once.
6Drawing a force

Graphical representation of a force

Because a force has magnitude, direction and a point of application, we can draw it as a single arrow (a straight line segment). Each part of the arrow carries one piece of information:

📏 Length = magnitude

A longer arrow means a bigger force. 10 N is drawn twice as long as 5 N.

➡️ Arrow head = direction

The way the arrow points shows the direction of the force.

⏺️ Starting dot = point of application

The dot at the arrow's start marks where the force acts on the object.

7Everyday physics

Using direction & point of application to make work easier

Once you understand force, you can work smarter. We do this instinctively every day:

🛒 Change the direction

Dragging a loaded cart with a low, horizontal rope forces you to bend awkwardly. Pull the rope at an angle instead and the cart still moves forward — but your body works far more comfortably.

🔧 Change the point of application

Pushing a cart low down means bending over. Fit a handle higher up and you change where the force is applied — now you can push standing upright.

The takeawayEveryday skill isn't only scientific knowledge — it's practical experience. But knowing that a force has a direction and a point of application helps you choose the easiest way to get a job done.
