The only guide you'll need · Ch.1

The Invisible World
That Runs the Planet

Microbes are too small to see — yet they make your yoghurt, heal your infections, feed your crops, and (sometimes) make you sick. Let's understand why, not just memorise the facts.

▶ Start learning Test myself first
1The big picture

What even is a microbe?

A micro-organism is simply a living thing too small to see with your naked eye — you need a microscope. That's the whole definition. Everything else follows from being tiny and simple.

Think of them as nature's "starter kit" life-forms

Because their bodies are so simple, microbes get three superpowers that shape this entire chapter. If you remember these three, most facts will make sense on their own:

1 · FastThey grow and reproduce extremely quickly — one bacterium can become millions overnight.
2 · ToughThey adapt to almost anywhere — hot springs, salt marshes, even petrol and diesel.
3 · SimpleSimple genes make them easy to harness — which is exactly why industry loves them.
Why this mattersEvery "application" later in the chapter — making yoghurt, fixing nitrogen, producing antibiotics — works because microbes are fast, tough, and simple. You're not memorising a random list; you're seeing the same three traits used in different jobs.

Most microbes are actually helpful to humans and the environment. Only some are harmful. Scientists sort them into five groups — let's meet the family.

2Meet the five families

The 5 groups of micro-organisms

Four are clearly alive: Bacteria, Fungi, Protozoa, Algae. The fifth — Viruses — sits on the fence between living and non-living. Tap each one to explore.

🦠

Bacteria the workhorses

Single-celled, microscopic, in every environment on Earth.

Intuition: Bacteria are the busy factory workers of the microbe world. They're everywhere, they come in different shapes (rods, spheres, spirals), and humans have quietly hired them for thousands of jobs — from curdling milk to mining metal.

Job / traitBacterium (example)
Milk & dairy productsLactobacillus bulgaricus
Vinegar productionAcetobacter aceti
Anthrax disease (harmful)Bacillus anthracis
Cholera disease (harmful)Vibrio cholerae

Animation · How bacteria multiply (binary fission)

1 cell
The "fast" superpower, visualisedOne cell splits into two, two into four, four into eight… This is why a tiny amount of yoghurt culture can turn a whole pot of milk overnight, and why an untreated infection spreads so quickly.
🍄

Fungi the decomposers

Unicellular or multicellular. Love moist surfaces (substrates).

Intuition: Fungi are the mould on old bread and the yeast in your dough. Unlike the others, some fungi grow big enough to see with the naked eye — a mushroom is a fungus's reproductive organ poking out. They thrive on moist surfaces because that's where they feed.

Where you meet itFungus (example)
Mould on breadMucor
Yeast (bread, alcohol)Saccharomyces
Easy to confuseBoth bacteria and fungi are used to make antibiotics, and both can spoil food. The key difference: fungi can be multicellular and sometimes visible (mushrooms); bacteria are always single-celled and microscopic.
🐛

Protozoa the tiny hunters

Unicellular, microscopic, and they move.

Intuition: Protozoa are like microscopic animals. Their signature trait is movement — they swim and crawl using special structures. Match the tool to the creature and they're easy to remember:

🌾 Cilia

Paramecium — tiny hairs that beat like oars.

🦶 Pseudopodia

Amoeba — "false feet", oozes along.

🏊 Flagella

Euglena — a whip-like tail.
The dangerous onePlasmodium is a protozoan that causes malaria. It lives in aquatic environments and inside other organisms — keep it in mind for the "diseases" section.
🌿

Algae the sun-eaters

Unicellular or multicellular. They contain chlorophyll.

Intuition: Algae are the "plants" of the microbe world. The one thing that sets them apart from the other four families: they have chlorophyll and can photosynthesise — they make their own food from sunlight. Microscopic algae floating on water are called phytoplankton.

FormAlga (example)
UnicellularChlamydomonas
Filamentous (threads)Spirogyra
Glassy shellsDiatoms
Exam trap"Which group is autotrophic (makes its own food)?" → Algae. That's the chlorophyll giveaway.
👾

Viruses the in-betweeners

Alive? Not alive? Both. Only seen with an electron microscope.

Intuition: A virus is like a USB stick of genetic code. On its own it's inert — no cells, no respiration, no growth. But plug it into a living cell and it hijacks the machinery to copy itself. That's why it straddles the line between living and non-living.

Interactive · The virus's split personality

🧊 On its own🧬 Inside a cell

Non-living mode: No cellular organisation. Doesn't respire, doesn't grow. Basically a chemical particle.

Disease causedVirus
Common cold / fluInfluenza virus
AIDSHIV
DengueDengue virus
Remember for the examViruses multiply only inside living cells, have no cellular organisation, and antibiotics do not work against them. All three facts come from the same idea: a virus isn't a full living cell.

3Habitats & substrates

They live absolutely everywhere

If there's an ecosystem on Earth, microbes are in it — soil, water, and up to 6 km high in the atmosphere. They live on and inside plants and animals too.

🏠 Substrates (their food & home)

A substrate is the surface a microbe grows on and feeds from. On your body and food, favourites include:

Meat · fish · fruits · vegetables · human skin · mouth · alimentary canal · urinary tract.

🔥 Extreme survivors

Some microbes shrug off conditions that would kill almost anything else:

Hot water springs · salt marshes · even liquids like petrol and diesel.

CallbackThis is superpower #2 — tough. Adaptability is why microbes are the most widespread living things on the planet.
4The central question

Friend or foe? Mostly friend.

Here's the mental map for the rest of the chapter. Every microbe fact fits into one of these two buckets. Keep this picture in your head:

✅ Beneficial effects

Humans use microbes on purpose in four fields:

AgricultureMedicineIndustryEnvironment

Mnemonic: "A MIE" — Agriculture, Medicine, Industry, Environment.

⚠️ Adverse effects

Microbes also cause harm in four ways:

DiseasePlant diseaseFood spoilageBio-weapons

Same organisms, different outcome — a pathogen is just a microbe doing harm.

5Beneficial effects · A M I E

How we put microbes to work

Four fields, one idea each. Understand the why behind each and the examples stick by themselves.

🌾 Agriculture — growing more, better food

Microbes help farms in four distinct ways. Notice each one solves a specific farming problem:

① Gene technology — upgrading the crop itself

Scientists borrow a helpful gene from a microbe and put it into a crop to give it a new ability.

ResultGene comes fromWhat it does
Golden Rice (extra vitamin A)Erwinia uredovoraRice enriched with vitamin A
Bt Maize (pest-proof)Bacillus thuringiensis → into Zea maizePlant makes its own toxin against crop pests

② Nitrogen fixation — free fertiliser from thin air

Here's the puzzle: 78% of the air is nitrogen, a nutrient plants desperately need — yet plants can't absorb it directly from the air. Certain bacteria solve this by "fixing" airborne nitrogen into a form roots can use.

Animation · Nitrogen fixation

N₂ gas78% of the air (plants can't use it)
➜
Rhizobiumbacteria in root nodules of legumes (beans, peas)
➜
Usable nitrogenabsorbed by the plant → bigger yield
Two ways it happensRhizobium lives in the root nodules of legumes. Azotobacter is free-living — added straight to soil.
Key termBio-fertilizers = substances that enrich soil with nutrients using micro-organisms (like commercially produced Rhizobium).

③ Compost — recycling dead matter into soil food

Bacteria and fungi rapidly decompose organic matter into compost, which adds minerals to soil and creates good growing conditions.

④ Bio-pesticides — microbes as natural pest control

Some microbes control pests instead of chemicals. Example: the fungus Alternaria is used to combat the aquatic weed salvinia.

💊 Medicine — fighting microbes with microbes

The clever twist: we use micro-organisms to treat diseases caused by other micro-organisms. Three tools:

🧪 Antibiotics

Chemicals a microbe makes to destroy another microbe. Made by fungi & bacteria.

💉 Vaccines

A weakened/killed microbe (or its toxin) that trains your body to defend itself.

🛡️ Anti-toxins

Vaccines made by removing the toxic part of a pathogen's toxin.
The #1 exam fact about antibioticsAntibiotics kill bacteria and fungi — but NOT viruses. So they're useless against a cold or dengue. And you shouldn't take them without medical advice, because misuse causes side effects.
AntibioticFights
Penicillin, Amoxicillin, Tetracycline, ErythromycinBacterial infections
GriseofulvinFungal infections

🎓 Extra: Penicillin was discovered by Alexander Fleming (Scotland), produced from the fungus Penicillium notatum.

Vaccines — four ways to make one

All vaccines contain something that resembles a disease-causing microbe so your body learns to fight it. They differ in how that "something" is prepared:

Made from…Examples
Weakened microbesPolio, Tuberculosis, Measles
Killed microbesCholera, Influenza, Typhoid
Toxins of microbesTetanus, Diphtheria
Body parts (genetic engineering)Hepatitis B

🏭 Industry — "Industrial Microbiology"

Using microbes for economic benefit in industry is called Industrial Microbiology. Why do industries love microbes? Because of our three superpowers: fast processes, cheap/free to obtain, need little energy, and cause minimal pollution.

IndustryMicro-organismQuick note
Alcohol & BakerySaccharomyces cerevisiae (yeast)Same yeast, two industries
VinegarAcetobacter aceti
Dairy (yoghurt, curd, cheese, butter)Lactobacillus bulgaricus, Streptococcus thermophilusTurn lactose → lactic acid
BiogasMethanococcus, MethanobacteriumAnaerobic → methane
Plant fibresBacillus corchorus, Bacillus comesiiPectinase digests pectate
Metal extractionAcidithiobacillus / Thiobacillus ferrooxidansBio-leaching

⚡ Biogas

Straw, cow dung & water + anaerobic bacteria (Methanococcus) → methane gas → clean energy source.

⛏️ Metal extraction (bio-leaching)

Using microbes to extract metal from low-grade ores. Extracts Uranium and Copper.

🧵 Plant fibres

Fibres (coconut, hemp) are glued together by pectate. The enzyme pectinase from bacteria digests it to free the fibres.

🥛 Dairy

Lactobacillus & Streptococcus turn lactose into lactic acid → acidic medium stops other microbes → natural preservation.

Step-through · How yoghurt is made (Activity 1.1)

🥛
Step 1 · Heat the milk
Heat cow's milk for 15–30 min at 88–95 °C to kill unwanted bacteria.
Why each temperature?Boiling kills the bad bacteria first. Then you cool to 60 °C before adding culture — too hot would kill the good Lactobacillus you're adding. Finally, refrigeration slows everything down to preserve it.

🌍 Environment — microbes as clean-up crew

Removing pollutants using micro-organisms is called bio-remediation. Think of these microbes as a natural clean-up team that eats the pollution.

Clean-up jobHow microbes help
Polluted waterMicrobes decompose organic waste matter
Ocean oil spillsPseudomonas releases an enzyme that decomposes hydrocarbons in oil
Heavy metals (Cr, Pb, Hg)Polluted water passed through a tower of bacteria that remove the toxic metals
Plastic wasteMicrobes produce bio-degradable plastics (decomposed by bacteria)
Don't mix these upBio-remediation = cleaning pollution. Bio-leaching = extracting metal from ore. Bio-fertilizer = enriching soil. Same "bio-" prefix, very different jobs.
6Adverse effects

The dark side: when microbes harm us

Four kinds of harm: human disease, plant disease, food spoilage, and biological weapons.

🤒 Causing disease — the chain of infection

To understand any disease, learn these three roles. It's always the same story:

🦠
PathogenThe microbe that causes the disease
➜
🦟
VectorThe carrier (mosquito, fly) that transports it
➜
🧍

Worked example · DenguePathogen = dengue virus. Vector = mosquito (it carries the virus). Host = the human, where symptoms appear. Note: not every disease needs a vector — airborne ones like the cold skip straight to the host.

Spot the patternHow it enters tells you how to prevent it: air → respiratory, food/water → digestive, bite → through skin. That's also the answer to "good health habits to avoid infection": clean water, covered food, mosquito control, personal hygiene.

🎓 Nice twist: the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (the same one used in Bt maize) is used as biological control to destroy dengue mosquito larvae — a microbe fighting a microbe's vector.

🌱 Diseases in plants

Powdery mildew

Caused by a fungus. White/grey powder on leaves, stems, flowers, fruit. Damages the whole plant.

Late blight

Caused by a fungus. Common in potato. Brown spots on leaves turn black, then spread.

Wilting

Caused by fungi or bacteria. Damages the xylem → poor water transport → plant withers.

🍞 Food spoilage

Food gives microbes everything they need to multiply. As they feed, they change the food's physical and chemical nature until it's unfit to eat — that's food spoilage.

What changes?

Physical: softer, colour, taste, odour change.
Chemical: fermentation of carbohydrates, putrefaction of proteins, rancidification of lipids.

What speeds it up?

Internal: nutrients, moisture, food's structure.
External: temperature, humidity, gases in the air.

Connect it backThis is why the yoghurt method uses refrigeration — cold is an external factor that slows microbial growth. Preservation is just food spoilage in reverse.

☠️ Biological weapons

Toxins from pathogenic bacteria or viruses used for military purposes are called biological weapons. The most dangerous one today: Bacillus anthracis, the cause of anthrax — harmful to humans, animals and plants.

7Last-minute revision

⚡ The night-before cheat sheet

Everything essential, condensed. Skim this the hour before your exam — if a point feels unfamiliar, tap back to the section that explains it.

🔑 Definition & 3 traits

  • Microbe = living thing too small for the naked eye.
  • Traits: fast reproduction, tough (adaptable), simple genes.
  • 5 groups: Bacteria, Fungi, Protozoa, Algae, Virus.

🧬 The 5 groups (signature trait)

  • Bacteria: unicellular, everywhere, many shapes.
  • Fungi: uni/multi, moist surfaces, some visible.
  • Protozoa: move via cilia/pseudopodia/flagella.
  • Algae: chlorophyll → photosynthesis (autotroph).
  • Virus: no cells, living+non-living, needs a host.

🌾 Agriculture

  • Golden Rice ← Erwinia uredovora (vitamin A).
  • Bt maize ← Bacillus thuringiensis (pest toxin).
  • Rhizobium = N-fixing in legume root nodules.
  • Azotobacter = free-living N-fixer (bio-fertilizer).
  • Alternaria = bio-pesticide vs salvinia weed.

💊 Medicine

  • Antibiotics kill bacteria & fungi, NOT viruses.
  • Bacterial: Penicillin, Amoxicillin, Tetracycline, Erythromycin.
  • Fungal: Griseofulvin.
  • Vaccines: weakened / killed / toxin / genetic.
  • Penicillin → Fleming, from Penicillium notatum.

🏭 Industry (Industrial Microbiology)

  • Alcohol & bakery = Saccharomyces (yeast).
  • Vinegar = Acetobacter aceti.
  • Dairy = Lactobacillus + Streptococcus (lactose→lactic acid).
  • Biogas = Methanococcus → methane.
  • Bio-leaching = metal from ore (Uranium, Copper).

🌍 Environment

  • Bio-remediation = removing pollution with microbes.
  • Pseudomonas → decomposes ocean oil spills.
  • Bacteria towers remove heavy metals (Cr, Pb, Hg).
  • Microbes make bio-degradable plastics.

🤒 Disease chain & key cases

  • Pathogen causes → Vector carries → Host suffers.
  • Virus: Cold, Dengue, AIDS. Bacteria: TB, Typhoid.
  • Protozoa: Malaria, Amoebic dysentery, Leishmaniasis.
  • Air→respiratory, food/water→digestive, bite→skin.

⚠️ Other harms

  • Plant: powdery mildew, late blight, wilting (xylem).
  • Spoilage: physical (colour/taste/odour) + chemical (ferment/putrefy/rancid).
  • Bio-weapon: Bacillus anthracis = anthrax.
Memory hooks:  • Beneficial fields = "A MIE" (Agriculture, Medicine, Industry, Environment).  • Antibiotics = "Bacteria & Fungi, never Viruses."  • The three "bio-" jobs: remediation cleans, leaching mines, fertilizer feeds.  • Disease = P→V→H (Pathogen, Vector, Host).
